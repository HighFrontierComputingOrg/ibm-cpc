# ADI Alternating Direction Implicit

Before electronic computers emerged, researchers could not easily model detailed fluid flow in subsurface formations, especially not using purely pencil-and-paper analysis. Early analytical frameworks relied on sweeping simplifications, treating an entire geologic reservoir as a uniform tank and balancing gross fluid withdrawals against reservoir pressure declines. While effective for initial volumetric estimates, these total balance methods were incapable of predicting localized spatial behavior such as water breaking prematurely into producing wells and gas or water coning upwards towards wells across permeable intervals. Confronted with these challenging and costly operational problems, the industry relied on physical models and hardware circuits where physical processes directly solved the governing flow equations. The earliest physical systems modeled fluid movement directly within scaled granular beds and transparent flow cells. Researchers packed transparent enclosures with graded sand, glass beads, or consolidated rock slabs, saturating the media with oil and injecting water or gas to visually record displacement fronts and localized coning phenomena. They also built parallel glass plates separated by narrow capillary gaps to study displacement mechanisms and viscous fingering instabilities. Despite their visual utility, physical models suffered from severe scaling conflicts. When a geological reservoir is reduced to laboratory benchtop proportions, microscopic capillary retention becomes unnaturally dominant relative to field dimensions. Overcoming this capillary resistance required pumping fluids at exaggerated velocities, which distorted the balance between viscous forces and gravity, producing poor results compared with actual subsurface behavior.

To bypass the scaling distortions of realistic fluids, researchers exploited mathematical analogies linking fluid flow in porous media to electrical conduction and heat transfer. In steady flows, fluid pressure maps directly to electrical voltage and flow rate behaves as electrical current. Electrolytic tanks, developed in the thirties and forties, consisted of shallow, non-conductive basins cast to mirror the structural contours, fault boundaries, and sand thicknesses of real subsurface formations. Filled with an electrolyte solution and fitted with metal electrodes representing producing and injecting wells, these tanks established continuous electric potential fields. By tracing equipotential lines across the liquid surface with voltage probes, it was possible to map fluid streamtubes and evaluate waterflood patterns. Because fluid-filled trays were limited to steady flow without fluid expansion or compression, researchers went on to develop discrete network analyzers. Instead of a continuous electrolyte bath, the reservoir volume was divided into discrete blocks linked by physical circuit elements. Electrical resistors modeled rock-fluid transmissibility, discrete capacitors stored charge to represent pore volume compressibility and fluid expansion, and variable current sources injected or withdrew charge to replicate well activity. Calibrating these room-sized network boards required manual history matching, where potentiometer and capacitor dials were iteratively adjusted until oscilloscope traces and voltmeters matched historical bottom-hole pressure surveys. Once calibrated, the circuit reproduced transient pressures continuously in real time, predicting water drive behavior and reservoir pressure decline.

By the fifties, physical and electrical analog devices reached an operational ceiling imposed by hardware rigidity and non-linear physics. Linear electrical components with standard resistors and capacitors could not adjust dynamically to model complex flow relationships, and whenever ongoing drilling revised a field's geological boundaries, model electrolyte basins had to be reshaped and discrete electrical networks rewired by hand. The arrival of programmable digital logic enabled researchers to replace rigid hardware installations with numerical grids, discretizing the reservoir into spatial arrays and solving the governing partial differential equations via linear algebraic matrices. By developing formulations that resolved pressure distributions implicitly across the grid and updated fluid saturations over discrete time intervals, digital software handled multi-phase flow, complex rock heterogeneities, and dynamic phase transitions, with room-sized wiring boards and benchtop displacement models replaced by software simulations running on mainframes.

When Donald Peaceman joined Production Research at the Humble Oil in 1951, computational reservoir modeling was limited to accounting-oriented electromechanical machinery. After experimenting during night shifts on an accounting department IBM 604 multiplying punch, Peaceman and Henry Rachford acquired an IBM CPC in 1952. The machine was not an integrated computer designed from the ground up, but rather a hybrid configuration cobbled together by computing groups in the aircraft industry and later commercialized. At its core, the CPC coupled an IBM 418 accounting machine serving as card reader, line printer operating at 150 lines per minute, and counter storage for eight 10-digit decimal numbers, with an IBM 605 electronic calculator unit, a card punch, and three auxiliary electromechanical storage boxes known as "ice boxes". Each ice box contained electromechanical counter wheels reminiscent of automobile odometers that held 16 ten-digit decimal numbers. Users could lift the lids during debugging sessions and visually verify the internal values. Although the CPC was factory-wired for fixed-point decimal arithmetic, which severely restricted non-linear scientific calculations, Peaceman and Rachford altered the installation by wiring large external plugboards to perform floating-point arithmetic. This arrangement executed two floating-point operations per punched card, providing an effective speed of five floating-point operations per second. Data memory remained capped at 56 ten-digit decimal words across the 418’s counter units and the three external storage boxes, and because programs were not stored internally in memory but read directly from punched card decks passing sequentially through the card reader, the effective length of a routine was bounded only by physical card stacks.

Peaceman and Rachford first applied this computing machinery to gas flow in both one-dimensional linear and radial depletion geometries. In physical practice on the CPC, the iterative programs required the operator to manually reload and pass the same card deck through the reader repeatedly, verifying numerical convergence line by line before inserting the subsequent deck for the next time interval. The 56-word working storage ceiling on the CPC governed the development and testing of the ADI Alternating Direction Implicit method when extending flow models to two spatial dimensions. Implementing a tridiagonal solution along any individual grid row or column sweep required retaining four numerical words per node. Dividing the 56 available words of internal storage by four yielded a maximum operable line sweep of 14 nodes, which directly established the dimensions of Peaceman and Rachford's standard 14 by 14 grid. Intermediate matrix values calculated along one coordinate sweep were punched out directly onto cards, which were then processed through an external electromechanical card sorter to rearrange the sequence for the orthogonal sweep. Because data had to enter the reader in reverse order along alternating coordinate directions, Peaceman and Rachford punched the values backwards and upside down, allowing the operator to invert the card deck manually to complete the directional cycle. This was very reminiscent of the Simplex work Dantzig was doing on the RAND CPC around the same time, where a matrix was inverted by flipping a card deck. Through this hardware-constrained workflow, the team verified flow in idealized reservoir geometries, laying the operational foundations of numerical reservoir simulation prior to the arrival of magnetic drum and core systems like the Bendix G-15 and IBM 704.

## Example

This example applies the alternating-direction method described by Peaceman
on pages 114–116 of [the 1990 reference](1990-peaceman.pdf) to the same
two-by-three problem used in the SOR example.

Each ADI iteration consists of two sweeps. The first solves a tridiagonal
system across each row, treating the horizontal direction implicitly and the
vertical direction explicitly. The second solves down each column, reversing
those roles. The boundary ring remains fixed, and each line system is solved
with NumPy. For this unit-spaced grid, `time_step` is the ADI
iteration parameter rather than a physical time increment.

From the repository root:

```sh
.venv/bin/python -m adi.adi
```

The script stops when the largest interior-value change over a complete pair
of sweeps is at most `1e-10`, then prints the grid to three decimal places.
The converged interior values are approximately:

```text
0.416  0.509  0.416
0.155  0.205  0.155
```
