# Simplex And Refinery Modeling

Refining quickly proved to be an ideal environment for immediate work using Simplex, and the growth there of computers and linear programming was extremely rapid. In Houston, John Bonner and Joe Moore were chemical engineers at Humble Oil’s Baytown refinery. With Simplex, by 1956 they had formed one of the earliest consulting firms dedicated to software applications and were leading the refining industry into the era of linear programming with early mainframes like the IBM 650. To trace these developments backwards, at Humble they had full access to communications across what would become Exxon, including those from Gifford Symonds. Symonds had been circulating technical memos about Simplex and refining since 1953, and in 1955 these were collected and published by the company in the landmark 74-page hardcover monograph “Linear Programming: The Solution of Refinery Problems”. This was not only one of the first practical books about modeling and simulation using computers, it was effectively one of the first about computational linear algebra and computer programming. By developing domain-specific software tools such as the Refinery and Petrochemical Modeling System, alongside the Gamma matrix generator and report writer, Bonner and Moore automated matrix assembly and translation, transforming the example problems and framework of Symonds into a standardized, daily computing practice across the refining industry.

Symonds was effectively the conduit to chemical engineers and refinery specialists of the advances around Simplex and linear programming, and he was actively collaborating with two leading researchers who would work at UT Austin from the seventies, Abraham Charnes and William Cooper. For the growth of Simplex in refining, they played a role as important as Dantzig’s, beginning with aviation gasoline blending in 1952 and modeling vertically-integrated operations in the oil industry in 1954. The story traces back to 1949, when the economist Cooper shared early drafts of Dantzig's Simplex method with the mathematician Charnes, and they simultaneously began working with Bob Mellon at Gulf Oil to eliminate "octane giveaway" in refinery blending operations. Their collaboration was defined by a commitment to let real-world operating complexities drive theoretical advances. In formulating the aviation gasoline blending model, Charnes and Cooper encountered the issue of mathematical degeneracy in Dantzig's algorithm, leading Charnes to formulate perturbation methods to ensure convergence. 

The period from the 1952 gasoline blending work of Charnes and Cooper to the 1955 book by Symonds saw the dawn of a major market for computer modeling in the refining industry, one that helped drive the commercial growth of the computer industry, with significant amounts of early computing power going directly into the oil industry. This work showed corporate management that linear programming was a practical tool with direct financial returns, creating the early commercial demand that justified major corporate investments in digital computers. Over the next several years Charnes and Cooper teamed with Symonds to pioneer methods for weather-sensitive heating oil distribution and resolving conflicting operating objectives. The relocation of Charnes and Cooper to UT Austin was orchestrated by the business and economics departments as part of creating a major center for quantitative analysis, computer modeling, and commercial technology transfer. In 1968 Charnes established the Center for Cybernetic Studies as a primary research hub for mathematical programming and logistics, with Cooper joining in 1980. Together again, they adapted linear programming to evaluate the efficiency of organizations without relying on artificial cost functions or market prices.

The maximum-profit running plan problem, adapted by Symonds from earlier work by Charnes and Cooper, captures the delicate balancing act at the heart of refinery management. A plant manager has four crude oils A, B, C, and D on hand, each arriving in limited weekly quantities. Crude C offers an operational choice: it can be routed through a standard fuel setup or tuned to extract specialized lube distillate, giving five possible ways to run the plant. Every run cracks the oil into a different mix of four finished products, gasoline, heating oil, lube distillate, and fuel oil, each carrying its own margin per barrel. The operational challenge lies in the boundaries. On the supply side, crude availability is strictly capped by weekly deliveries. On the market side, customer demand sets an upper limit on how much of each fuel can be sold. Crucially, these product targets are ceilings rather than mandatory quotas. If processing another barrel yields low-margin products that fail to cover their costs, the refinery simply leaves that demand unmet rather than chasing volume for its own sake.

To navigate these trade-offs, the Simplex method begins with the plant entirely idle, running no crude and leaving all market demand unfilled. At each step, it answers a fundamental question: which operation adds the most profit per barrel? The algorithm ramps up that run until it strikes an immediate physical bottleneck, either an empty crude tank or a saturated product market. Once a constraint binds, the method brings in the next most attractive run to rebalance throughput, continually substituting one operational path for another. When no further substitution can improve the margin, the refinery settles into an optimal rhythm. Every barrel of market demand for gasoline, heating oil, and lube distillate is satisfied, while leaving a slight shortfall of barrels in fuel oil where additional processing would erode net returns. The resulting balance generates roughly $67,830 in weekly profit, offering an early, definitive demonstration of how linear programming could turn raw operational constraints into disciplined economic strategy.
