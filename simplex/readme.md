Refining quickly proved to be an ideal environment for immediate work using Simplex and the growth of computers and linear programming was extremely rapid. In Houston, John Bonner and Joe Moore were chemical engineers at Humble Oil’s Baytown refinery. With Simplex, by 1956 they had formed their own consulting firm dedicated to pioneering work with early mainframes like the IBM 650. To trace these developments backwards, at Humble they had full access to communications across what would become Exxon, including those from Gifford Symonds. Symonds had been circulating technical memos about Simplex and refining since 1953, and in 1955 these were collected and published by the company in the landmark 74-page hardcover monograph “Linear Programming: The Solution of Refinery Problems”. This was not only one of the first practical books about modeling and simulation using computers, it was effectively one of the first about applied linear algebra and computer programming. By developing domain-specific software tools such as the Refinery and Petrochemical Modeling System, alongside the Gamma matrix generator and report writer, Bonner and Moore automated matrix assembly and translation, transforming the example problems and framework of Symonds into a standardized, daily computing practice across the refining industry.

Symonds was effectively the conduit to chemical engineers and refinery specialists of the work surrounding Simplex, and he was collaborating directly with two leading researchers who would move to UT Austin by the seventies, Abraham Charnes and William Cooper. They played a role as important as Dantzig’s for the growth of Simplex in refining, beginning with their landmark 1952 work on aviation gasoline blending and their 1954 work on modeling vertically-integrated operations in the oil industry. The period from the 1952 work of Charnes and Cooper to the 1955 book by Symonds saw the dawn of a major market for computer modeling in refining that helped drive the commercial growth of the computer industry, with significant amounts of the early computing power going directly into the oil industry.

The maximum-profit running-plan problem from Symonds third chapter (1955) and Charnes (1954) is the example. Each week, the refinery can process crudes A, B, C, and D, but only up to the amounts available; crude C can be sent through either of two operations, C1 or C2. Each operation produces a different mix of four products, G, H, L, and F, and earns a different profit per barrel. Product targets cap production; they are not mandatory minimums, so the model can leave demand unmet and records the shortfall when it is not profitable to make more. Simplex starts with a feasible plan that runs no crude, then repeatedly brings in the operation that most improves profit. A ratio test identifies which crude supply or product target becomes limiting; the corresponding tableau pivot gives a new feasible plan. When no pivot can improve the profit, the resulting plan runs A, B, C1, C2, and D at 37.5, 100, 100, 100, and about 58.3 Mbpw. It meets the targets for G, H, and L, leaves 3.75 Mbpw of F unmet, and earns about $67,833 per week (the book reports $67,830 from its rounded tableau).
